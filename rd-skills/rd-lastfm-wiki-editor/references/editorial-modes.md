# Editorial modes, depth, and subject rules

Read when deciding which task to execute, expanding an existing entry, or continuing a multi-message research project.

## Intent and output routing

- **Standard wiki:** research, verify, draft, link, lint, then output only final BBCode.
- **Research verification:** identify support, discrepancies, and gaps, with trustworthy citations and/or source URLs. Use normal explanatory prose; do not force BBCode.
- **Existing-wiki improvement:** fact-check supplied text; preserve accurate useful content, correct errors, improve flow and BBCode, add important verified information, remove repetition. By default return revised BBCode only. If asked to *audit* rather than rewrite, provide findings and recommendations first, without gratuitously rewriting.
- **Depth adjustment:** preserve scope and facts while adapting length. If user says “shorten this,” do not erase essential identity, major facts, or corrected details.
- **Conversation:** user may ask about a historical claim, paste more information, or negotiate style. Answer the specific question and maintain the working context instead of regenerating unrequested wiki text.

The modes can combine. A user can ask for a verification report plus a comprehensive revised wiki. Separate explanatory findings from the BBCode; never inject citations into the published portion.

## Depth settings

- **Brief:** compact identifying background and essential release/career context; usually no headings.
- **Standard (default):** balanced history and significant documented developments, more than a metadata summary but without filler.
- **Comprehensive:** research-intensive chronological and topical detail, including relevant context and supported recording/release histories; use headings when useful, not by rote.
- **Focused expansion:** expand only the requested area (e.g., recording sessions, origins, later releases), retaining unrelated accurate passages unless coherence requires a small change.

No rigid word-count targets. The available evidence determines maximum useful depth. Do not pad an obscure act just to hit “comprehensive.”

## Specific subject rules

### Artists

Start with the act's identity, documented origin, and broad musical identity. Describe formation and progression, notable original releases, verified collaborations, and meaningful changes where supported. Organize chronologically unless another structure improves clarity. Do not include a current/former-members roster. Mention individuals only when essential to explain a documented event or credited recording contribution. Do not assert a break-up, reunion, or ongoing activity without evidence.

### Albums

Identify the recording's primary artist, type (studio album, EP, live album, compilation, soundtrack, etc.), and original release context. Where verified, discuss label, recording sessions and production, significant tracks/guests, and links to the surrounding discography. A later deluxe edition or reissue is not the original issue. Do not output the complete tracklist unless explicitly requested, and do not write a review.

### Tracks

Identify the precise recording and artist, where it first appeared, relevant single/album release, documented credits, guest performers, and recording context. Discuss lyrics and intended themes only when supported; do not invent interpretation or reproduce lyrics. A cover, remix, or live performance should not silently inherit the original recording's credits.

### Record labels

Identify founding/background, geographic and historical context, releasing/distribution activities, associated music styles, and major *verified* catalogue milestones. Avoid sales copy, unsupported significance claims, and generic artist-roster lists.

## Editorial style

- Third person throughout published entries. Research conversation need not be third person.
- Write connected paragraphs. No infoboxes, one-line origin/genre attribute lists, promotional taglines, or bullet-heavy discographies.
- Accurate proper nouns and title capitalization take precedence over stylistic normalization.
- Link relevant entities but avoid visually cluttering the prose with repeated tags.
- Prefer plain, precise wording to subjective labels such as “legendary,” “iconic,” “brilliant,” or “masterpiece.”
- Attribution of notable critical reception is acceptable only when it is directly relevant, accurately sourced, and not a surrogate for the writer's own opinion.
- Optional section headings are `[b]Background[/b]`, `[b]Career and Releases[/b]`, `[b]Recording and Release[/b]`, etc. Choose headings that accurately reflect the content; omit them for short articles.

## Revision method

1. Read the supplied wiki as **unverified input**.
2. Identify independently corroborated facts, doubtful claims, missing context, weak structure, and broken links.
3. Resolve material problems with research.
4. Preserve verified facts and useful structure; make targeted changes unless a comprehensive rewrite was requested or necessary.
5. Compare the revision with the original to ensure useful factual details were not lost.
6. Output in the agreed format; optionally provide an external change log only when requested.

## Context across follow-ups

Maintain the same subject identity, verified claims, selected depth, and explicit editorial preferences for successive revisions. If a user provides new evidence, treat it as a lead, evaluate provenance, and revise only the affected claims. If asked about a single release date, answer it rather than rewriting the article. Do not ask for information already supplied in this conversation.
